Safety in the field: a note from the Example Field Robotics Group

The Example Field Robotics Group was started in 2011 by a small team of students who wanted to take robots out of the laboratory and into orchards, quarries, river banks and building sites. In the early years the group owned one tracked vehicle, a borrowed laptop and a shed. Today it looks after a fleet of ground and surface vehicles, a workshop with its own electronics bench, and a growing library of data collected on real sites. Most of the people who have passed through the group have gone on to work in agriculture, construction, mapping and environmental monitoring, and several have started their own companies.

Working outdoors brings problems that indoor labs rarely meet. Mud and dust get into connectors. Sunlight blinds cameras at the wrong moment. A radio link that works perfectly in the car park fails behind a hill. A vehicle that is safe on level ground can tip over on a slope that looked gentle from a distance. Over the years the group has kept a written record of every incident, however small, and has learned a great deal from reading that record together. Most of the lessons are simple. Check the batteries before leaving. Walk the route first. Keep people out of the zone where the machine moves. Always carry a way of stopping the robot that does not depend on the robot's own computer.

Students often ask where they can learn these habits. Until now the answer has been to join a field trip and watch. That works, but it reaches only a few people each year, and the lessons depend on who happens to be on the trip. Several members of the group have therefore been writing a short course that gathers the practices in one place. It uses real examples from the incident record, with the names removed, and it asks participants to work through each one: what went wrong, what warning signs were missed, and what a safer plan would have looked like.

The course also covers the legal side of testing robots outdoors, including how to ask a landowner for permission, what to say to neighbours who may be curious or worried, and how to report an accident. A short section explains how to write a risk assessment that people will actually read, rather than one that is filed and forgotten.

The course will be run as a two-day workshop. It is open to students, technicians and staff who take part in outdoor tests, and to colleagues from other groups who are thinking of starting. No experience with robots is needed, although some experience of working outdoors will help. Participants should wear sturdy shoes and bring clothing for the weather, because part of the second day is spent in the yard behind the building. Lunch and tea will be provided on both days. A certificate of attendance will be given at the end.
